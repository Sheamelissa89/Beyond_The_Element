States of Matter

Temperature and pressure help determine whether matter is solid, liquid, or gas. Changing its state does not change an element's identity.

Explore the History of Matter →

Element examples below refer to approximately 20°C and ordinary atmospheric pressure. These are examples rather than complete lists.

Solids

A solid has a definite shape and volume. Its particles remain close together and move around relatively fixed positions.

Element examples

  • Iron — Fe
  • Copper — Cu
  • Carbon — C
  • Sulfur — S

Most elements are solids under these conditions.

Liquids

A liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container. Its particles remain close together while moving past one another.

Elements at these conditions

  • Mercury — Hg, a metal
  • Bromine — Br, a nonmetal

Water is also a liquid under these conditions, but it is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen.

Gases

A gas has no definite shape or volume. Its particles spread out to fill their container.

Element examples

  • Hydrogen — H
  • Nitrogen — N
  • Oxygen — O
  • Helium — He

Hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen commonly occur as two-atom molecules: H₂, N₂, and O₂.

Beyond the Familiar: Plasma

Plasma contains freely moving charged particles, including electrons and ions. It appears in stars, lightning, and some illuminated signs.

Plasma is a state of matter, rather than a separate classification of chemical elements.

Connect State and Classification

Mercury is both a metal and a liquid. Oxygen is both a nonmetal and a gas. State describes the physical condition; classification describes a group of element properties.
